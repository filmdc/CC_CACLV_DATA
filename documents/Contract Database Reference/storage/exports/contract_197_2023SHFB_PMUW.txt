Significant changes

In March of 2022, Second Harvest welcomed Allison Czapp as its Associate Executive Director. Since
taking the helm, the organization has increased our impact – both in the number of people served and
total pounds distributed. Under Ms. Czapp’s leadership, Second Harvest has also sharpened its focus
on using a more data-driven approach to program development. From August to November 2022,
Second Harvest administered our “Neighbor Experience Survey” on-site at food pantries to 1,200
participants. The analyzed results from the survey will inform our strategic planning and program
provision.
Second Harvest Food Bank also launched several new initiatives and pilots in 2022 to explore ways of
better serving our community. We are trialing an “order ahead” system at several pantries that allow
customers to select what pantries they would like from on online menus; we launched our mobile pantry
program to alleviate food insecurity in areas of especially high-need; and we continue to build
relationships with schools to provide food pantries as part of other wrap-around services.
Significant accomplishments
During the last program year, Second Harvest:
• distributed 11 million pounds of food, including,
• 4.6 million pounds of fresh produce, perishable protein, and fresh dairy products distributed (a 24%
increase from the prior year),
• rescued 5.7 million pounds of food rescued that would otherwise end up in landfills,
• distributed 24,297 boxes of nutritious food to low-income seniors through the PA Senior Food Box
Program,
• provided 360 military families with a supply of shelf-stable and fresh foods each month through the
MilitaryShare program (double the amount served last year), and
• offered the Healthy Pantry Initiative, a partnership program with Feeding Pennsylvania, at 13 food
pantry sites. The program’s goal is to increase access and consumption of nutritious foods while also
making policy, systems and environmental changes at the pantry level to reduce nutrition insecurity and
improve health equity.
Significant challenges, including responding/adapting to COVID-19
This year has been difficult for Second Harvest and the people we serve. The year began with lingering
uncertainty about the COVID-19 pandemic, as people across America braced themselves for the end of
safety-net programs designed to prevent severe economic hardships. February 2022 is when we began
to see the number of people visiting pantries for the first time rising, as programs were allowed to expire,
and people were stung by inflation that reached levels not seen since the 1980s.
The demand for more food has put a lot of pressure on Second Harvest at a time when we are also
being impacted by rising food and fuel costs. Over the past year, orders from our pantry network
doubled and tripled in size. We are working hard to sustain our distribution levels to meet the need, but
there is a daunting road ahead. Perishable proteins, specifically, have been difficult to increase because
the cost of these products has increased dramatically, and unforeseen events – such as the avian
influenza epidemic – have affected supply.
During the past year, the number of individuals served by our network increased by 70%, while the
pounds of food we distributed rose only 30%. Our member agencies continue to serve an average of
80,000 people each month.
Challenges have also been exacerbated by staff turnover, warehouse staffing shortages, and general
burnout. We have faced stressful situations within our office and warehouse as we work to meet the
needs of our agency partners and people facing food insecurity.
Staffing issues over the past year have resulted in a pause to some program development efforts, but
we are eager to report that we are currently fully-staffed with new and existing staff members poised to
attend training and foodbanking network opportunities.

We are in the process of fully analyzing our Neighborhood Experience Survey, completed in December
2022. Our initial analysis that 75% of pantry customers in Monroe County said they ate fresh fruits and
vegetables less often than once per day. Despite the low rate of fresh produce consumption, more than
half (52%) of customers said they has special dietary needs due to chronic health disorders. Seventy-
three percent of customers with diet-related diseases would benefit from increased consumption of fresh
produce. About a third of customers said they “Always” or “Often” get enough fruits and vegetables at
the pantry they visit. These customers also reported higher rates of fresh produce consumption
compared with those who only “Sometimes” or “Rarely” received enough produce (48% of customers).
What barriers to data collection does your organization face? Examples could include lack of
centralized system, limited staff capacity, concerns about confidentiality, discomfort in asking
certain questions, etc.
At Second Harvest, we recognize that in order to make the most informed decisions regarding program
development and service provision, we must place an emphasis on data collection. In summer 2023, we
will be piloting Feeding America’s Service Insights program with a cohort of our food pantries. The
Service Insights program provides the necessary technology to collect data efficiently and without
creating burden on the pantry representative or the participant. We plan to scale up utilization of Service
Insights with the eventual goal of onboarding each agency within the Second Harvest network. Access
to timely data will deeply impact our ability to understand the people we serve. Service Insights will
greatly help to eliminate barriers to data collection.
Current barriers to data collection include:
• Limited staff/volunteer capacity at the pantry level,
• Limited staff capacity at Second Harvest to train pantry volunteers in preferred data collection
practices,
• Lack of technology at the pantry level to easily collect and organize participant data, and
• Fear that a participant might not return for additional needed food if they feel alienated from having to
answer too many questions.
Is there a specific demographic group that is under-represented or over-represented in the
group you serve? For example, "This program serves all ages but 80% of clients are 18-24."
Thirty-one percent of people served in Monroe County are children younger than 18 years old. Seniors
comprise an additional 21% of people served in the county. According to data from Feeding America,
6.44% of Monroe County residents are food insecure, but disparities exist among African-American
households (12% food insecure) and Hispanic households (12.8% food insecure). People with
disabilities also face higher rates of food insecurity in the county (13.4%).

Our program addresses the Priority Need: Basic Needs – Food. Second Harvest will address the need
by providing fresh produce for distribution in Monroe County.
A pre-pandemic study found that following the USDA’s MyPlate Dietary Guidelines would cost a family of
four between $1,000-$1,200 a month ($12,000-$14,400 annually) depending on the age of the family
members. For a comparison, the average middle-income family in the United States spends roughly
$6,224 on food each year with the average lower-income family spending even less at roughly $3,862
per year.1 Eating healthy food consistently is simply out of reach for many families.
The current inflation has exacerbated the situation. According to the USDA, the cost of groceries rose
11.8% from December 2021 to December 2022. The cost of specific items rose even higher – the price
of eggs increased by 60%.
We know that when money is tight, often the first thing cut from the family budget is fresh, healthy
groceries. And, with food prices elevated and still climbing, the sad truth is that many families are forced
to go without healthy foods. Second Harvest provides food to 23 emergency food sites within Monroe
County. Through partnership with our member agen-cies, we will work to ensure that all people have
access to the foods needed to keep them-selves and their families healthy. If a family can visit our food
pantries and bring home gro-ceries at no-cost, it will free up money to be spent elsewhere, meaning that
families might not be faced with the tough choice of buying food or paying the rent or utility bill.

Second Harvest uses numerous data sources to identify and document needs. As a member of the
Feeding America network, we have access to a variety of data, including the Map the Meal Gap, the
Meals per Person in Need (MPIN), and the data Tableau site. In addition, we use data from local Health
Needs Assessments, U.S. Census’ American Community Survey, the USDA, and the Food Research &
Action Center to help us determine the needs in our community.
What this data indicates in Monroe County is as follows. Feeding America’s Map the Meal Gap indicated
that in 2020, 10.4% of the Monroe County population (17,580 people), including 18.9% of children
(6,270), were food insecure. The food insecurity rate had increased from 9.4% in 2019. The U.S.
Census American Community Survey Five-Year Estimates (2017-2021) indicate that the individual
poverty rate for Monroe County is 10.6% and 14.5% for children; 14.3% of households have relied on
SNAP benefits in the previous 12 months; and the unemployment rate is 6.3%.
In fiscal year 2021-2022, Second Harvest distributed approximately 2.95 million pounds of food in
Monroe County, which translates into over 2.46 million meals. Nearly 1.2 million of those pounds were
fresh, perishable food.
Lehigh Valley Health Network’s 2022 Community Health Needs Assessment Health Profile in Monroe
County reported that 33% of Monroe County adults are considered obese (which is higher than the
national average of 29.5%). LVHN’s Health Needs Assessment also indicates that 86% of Monroe
County residents enrolled in Medicare have a diabetes diagnosis. The high incidence of obesity and
diabetes may be due, in part, to lack of access to fresh and healthy foods. The U.S. Department of
Agriculture further reports that there are two low-income, low food access areas, as well as several
limited supermarket areas in more rural parts of Monroe County.
Additionally, during the months of August through November 2022, Second Harvest administered our
“Neighbor Experience Survey” on-site at food pantries across the Second Harvest network. Monroe
County residents comprised 246 of the responses. The survey was administered with the goal of gaining
a better understanding of the people we serve and their relationship with their local pantry, and to
identify ways to strengthen our network.
Results show that Monroe County pantry participants are not consuming adequate amounts of fresh
fruits or vegetables. When asked, “During the past week, how many times did you eat fresh fruits?”,
41% of Monroe County participants responded with, “1-3 times per week”, and 13% responded with, “I
did not eat any fresh fruits in the past week.” A parallel question was asked regarding fresh vegetable
consumption. 47% of Monroe County survey participants reported consuming fresh vegetables only 1-3
times per week, and 13% reported they did not eat any fresh vegetables in the past week.
It is also important to note that 63% of respondents in Monroe County reported having one or more
dietary restrictions, with the majority saying they need foods that help them manage diabetes, such as
fresh fruits and vegetables.
These data indicate a need for access to fresh, healthy food in Monroe County.

Approach and Results
Describe the proposed program and how it will achieve the goal and outcome(s) that you
describe in the Performance Measurement Form.
At Second Harvest, we have applied a Fresh Forward approach to all aspects of our operation –
sourcing, producing, distributing, and educating. Our Fresh Forward approach entails prioritizing the
sourcing of fresh, healthy foods for distribution to food insecure households; innovating distribution
strategies to help more people access food, such as though our mobile and school pantry programs;
leveraging our resources at The Seed Farm to grow food for pantries and establish community gardens
in urban areas; and educating neighbors about the importance of healthy eating by providing them tools
to make healthier choices.
Funds from Pocono Mountain United Way will support the sourcing and distribution components of our
Fresh Forward approach. Second Harvest prioritizes the sourcing of fresh, nutritionally dense food,
while centering health and racial equity in our work. We recently overhauled our approach to food
sourcing and distribution, in an effort to maintain inventory that is at least 85% Foods to Encourage
(F2E) – Feeding America’s guideline for categorizing and ranking the nutritional quality of food. Our
nutrition and inventory control staff rank all food that is logged into Primarius, our inventory database
and the tool by which pantry coordinators place their food orders. The ranking system gives our pantry
coordinators greater detail and insight into the nutritional content of the foods they order for their
participants.
Pantry coordinators place their orders, which are then picked and delivered by Second Harvest staff.
Second Harvest works with a network of 200+ agencies to distribute fresh food to neighbors facing food
insecurity. We work to understand where unmet need exists and build community partnership to meet
those needs. And, we assist in shoring up the infrastructure where it is lacking through our mobile pantry
program. Last year, Second Harvest distributed 11 million pounds of food, 4.5 million of which were
fresh produce, perishable protein, and fresh dairy products.
The demand for fresh, healthy food continues to rise. Funding from the Pocono Mountain United Way
will make it possible for Second Harvest to distribute fresh produce to our network of 23 pantries in
Monroe County. Collectively, these pantries serve more than 17,600 people each month.

Second Harvest’s Fresh Forward distribution strategy includes:
• Orders prepared and scheduled for regular delivery by Second Harvest staff,
• “Stop and Drop” deliveries,
• Mobile pantry distributions, and
• “Free Friday” large scale distribution to pantries.
Utilizing Primarius, Second Harvest food pantry member agencies place their order online, that order is
then picked and scheduled for delivery. Food pantries are responsible for identifying volunteers to help
unload the delivery and bring the food into the pantry site for storage.
Our “Stop and Drop” initiative functions as a regular delivery, except it is delivered immediately before a
pantry’s scheduled distribution hours. “Stop and Drop” deliveries consist of fresh and perishable foods.
Delivering the foods during the time the pantry is open for distribution means that the food does not
need to enter the pantry building – eliminating the need for on-site cold storage. “Stop and Drop” is an
effective method of ensuring that pantry participants have direct access to greater quantities and
varieties of fresh, healthy foods.
In places where food pantry infrastructure is lacking, Second Harvest administers its Mobile Pantry
program. Through this initiative, utilizing our fleet of refrigerated trucks, we reach people directly in the
neighborhoods in which they live. Operating our mobile pantry at convenient times and locations that
allow for drive-thru distributions, we are able to provide large quantities of fresh, healthy foods very
efficiently.
“Free Friday” is a long-standing program of Second Harvest. It is a mass of distribution of perishable
products that pantries have the option of picking up in Monroe County at Stroudsburg Wesleyan Church
or from the Second Harvest warehouse. “Free Friday” is a method by which food pantries can accept
large quantities of perishable foods to take back to their site.
Our four-pronged approach to distributing fresh, healthy foods to Monroe County will result in more
individuals and families gaining access to the foods needed to maintain good health outcomes.

Our affiliation with Feeding America allows us to tap into additional resources that help support our daily

Describe your organization's capacity and experience to implement the proposed program and
meaningfully address the selected Priority Need.
Second Harvest, a program of Community Action Lehigh Valley, began its mission to end food insecurity
in 1982, as the Lehigh Valley Food Bank. Initially, distributions were made from the back of a rented
truck to a network of about 15 nonprofit agencies. Since September 2015, Second Harvest Food Bank
has been operating from a 65,000 square-foot warehouse in East Allen Township, which includes 96,000
cubic feet of space for refrigerated and frozen foods. Second Harvest operates a fleet of five 26-foot
trucks, two sprinter vans, and one 12-foot box truck. All but the box truck are refrigerated, meaning
Second Harvest can transport food in compliance with food safety regulations.
Second Harvest is the only Feeding America partner food bank serving Monroe County. Last year,
Second Harvest distributed 11 million pounds of food to 200+ nonprofit agencies, including 23 agencies
within Monroe County. Annually, Second Harvest distributes nearly 2.2 million pounds of food to Monroe
County.
Second Harvest has distributed food to meet the needs of the community for more than 40 years. There
has never been a pause to our distribution efforts, even during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic.
