Organization Information
Mission Statement
Community Action Lehigh Valley (CALV) administers programs that meet basic social needs while encouraging self-sufficiency. We establish partnerships with community organizations, local government,
and the private sector as we advance the economic and community development of low-income neighborhoods. Our mission is to improve the quality of life by building a community in which all people have
access to economic opportunity, the ability to pursue that opportunity, and a voice in the decisions that affect their lives. Our areas of impact include: advocacy; business start-up and development; food
access and nutrition; housing; neighborhood revitalization; and youth.
CALV offers food for the hungry through the Second Harvest Food Bank, as well as short- and long-term shelter for families experiencing homelessness at the Sixth Street Shelter, Turner Street Apartments
and Ferry Street Apartments. The Seed Farm is working to cultivate the next generation of farmers through training and incubator programs. The Housing Counseling Program is a HUD- and PHFA-
approved housing counseling agency that increases self-sufficiency, financial literacy, and asset-building by providing education, counseling, and assistance regarding homeownership, predatory lending, and
mortgage foreclosure. CALV’s Weatherization Program offers solutions to home heating problems through direct financial assistance, counseling, and weatherization of residences. The Campaign for Racial
and Ethnic Justice, through Generation Next, provides support to underserved students to help them achieve college admission, integrate into college life, and complete a post-secondary program. Racial
and Ethnic Justice also offers the Color Outside the Lines initiative, which engages the Lehigh Valley in a conversation about race and ethnicity, facilitates implicit bias trainings, and provides youth programs
in partnership with local school districts. SHE (She Has Everything) is an educational and inspirational program in which girls build self-esteem, are empowered to change the dynamics in their personal
relationships and the greater community, and seize control of their futures, ensuring healthy, fulfilling, and self-sufficient lives. Slate Belt Rising is a multi-municipality initiative that includes Wind Gap,
Bangor, Pen Argyl and Portland in an effort to promote the four boroughs and improve the lives of residents by revitalizing the Slate Belt. Each of these programs acts as a non-duplicative resource for the
Lehigh Valley.
CALV is also committed to economic and community development because of the belief that people have the best chance of reaching their full potential when their communities are places where people
want to live, work, and play. CALV’s economic and community development work is conducted by its subsidiary corporations. Community Action Development Allentown and Community Action
Development Bethlehem work with residents in establishing new businesses and revitalizing their communities. The Rising Tide Community Loan Fund is a federally certified community development
financial institution that provides microloans and technical and marketing assistance to small businesses. Community Action Homes is a comprehensive housing revitalization initiative that increases the
affordable housing stock in the Lehigh Valley through acquisition/rehab/resale, owner-occupied rehab, and rehab of residential and commercial facades. Lehigh Valley Community Land Trust increases the
supply of permanent affordable housing for low- to moderate-income households by working with Community Action Homes.
Community Served
Community Action Lehigh Valley (CALV) works with and for low-income residents of the Lehigh Valley in order to combat poverty. The Lehigh Valley, located 90 miles west of New York City and 60 miles
north of Philadelphia, is the third largest metropolitan area in Pennsylvania and the fastest growing, with an increasingly diverse population. This is particularly evident in more densely populated areas of
the three major cities (Allentown, Bethlehem, Easton); according to the American Community Survey 5-Year Estimates (ACS 2017-2021), 54.5% of the population in the City of Allentown identify as
Hispanic or Latino, a 30% increase since 2000, and residents who identify as Black or African American constitute 14.1% of the City’s population, a 6% increase since 2000. 17.8% of Lehigh Valley residents
five years and older speak languages other than English at home, and this rises to 47.5% in Allentown.
Across the Lehigh Valley, 12.3% of families with children under 18 are living in poverty, and this number rises to 31.7% for single mothers with children under 18 (ACS 2017-2021). According to Feeding
America, one in ten people and one in six children in our service area are food insecure, meaning they don’t have adequate access to food that is safe, nutritious, and consistent with preferences. Housing
insecurity is another major challenge with 49.9% of Lehigh Valley renters and 25% of homeowners with a mortgage cost-burdened (ACS 2017-2021), which means they spend at least 30% of their income on
housing costs. Affordable housing is a prime concern in the region. A recent report from RentCafe named the Lehigh Valley the second most competitive rental market in the country for small rental markets.
With a vacancy rate of about 3% in the region, rental prices have skyrocketed. Additionally, the City of Bethlehem reported that the median home sales price in Bethlehem rose from $142,500 in 2015-2017
to $203,866 in 2020-2022, a 43% increase. The Lehigh Valley is a desirable place to live, drawing new residents, but many low- to moderate-income residents are being squeezed out by rising housing costs.

Project Description
Located in Center City Allentown, the Sixth Street Shelter provides safe, short-term housing for low-income families with at least one child under the age of 18 experiencing homelessness. The property has
25 private apartments, a Family Resource Center, playground, and full-choice food pantry for residents. The Sixth Street Shelter is unique because it is the only shelter in the area where entire families of
any configuration (including those with fathers and teenaged boys, multi-generational families, and those from the LGBTQ+ community) stay together in their own private apartments.
It is essential that we provide families with a clean, safe, and well-maintained environment. On average, each apartment at the Sixth Street Shelter houses four to six families each year, and this leads to
immense wear and tear on the units. Deep cleaning and painting are common needs as vacated units are prepared for new families to move in, and furnishings and appliances must also be replaced
periodically. We are replacing damaged and worn furniture with items that are bed bug resistant and easy to clean and sanitize without causing damage to the furniture. Not only is this furniture durable
enough to handle the wear and tear of multiple families residing in the unit each year, but it also provides a safer, healthier environment for families in terms of cleaning and sanitizing against the spread of
viruses and bed bugs.
A $10,000 Kentucky Fried Wishes Grant would enable us to refresh three apartments and refurbish them with new appliances and bed bug resistant furnishings. This gives families a clean, safe, and
comfortable place to call home during their stay at the Sixth Street Shelter. Having a kitchen with their own refrigerator, stove and microwave in their apartment allows families to prepare meals and eat
together. The kitchen also provides a space for gathering to converse, do homework, and complete creative projects – all important forms of bonding for a family as they navigate the many challenges that
come with experiencing homelessness. The project will be considered successful when we are able to provide these safe, supportive spaces to our families.
For many families, the stay at the Sixth Street Shelter enables them to begin to stabilize their income and address the problems that led to their homelessness. The families who come to the Shelter do so for
a variety of reasons: low-paying jobs, lack of affordable housing, mental and/or physical health crises, domestic violence, and lack of access to reliable transportation, healthcare, and/or childcare. The
Shelter provides each family with a fully furnished apartment and supportive services, including individualized case management. Staff work to connect them with the necessary resources to address their
individual needs, such as financial literacy and parenting classes, access to childcare and healthcare, and mental health support. During their residency, the family develops a Family Service Plan with their
caseworker, setting household, budgeting, educational, and vocational goals and seeking suitable housing and employment. We have started a new Benchmark Family Care Initiative, which aims to ensure
that all residents have or obtain medical insurance and are connected to appropriate providers so that they can achieve a list of benchmarks related to their relevant medical, dental, and mental health care,
as well as track financial goals. Other recent initiatives include adding a Housing Innovation Specialist to our staff to work with our residents and local landlords in order to identify affordable housing
options and facilitate faster placements for families, and expanding our educational and vocational programming at the Sixth Street Shelter to provide residents with tools and credentials that will help them
to remain stably housed when they move out of the Shelter and into permanent housing. Staff maintain contact with the family following their move into permanent housing to offer additional assistance
and determine how well they are continuing to move toward self-sufficiency.
These services are critically important due to the detrimental impacts of homelessness on children. The First Focus Campaign for Children summarizes, “Studies show that housing instability and
homelessness exert long-term adverse physiological, academic, and economic effects on children.” Research from the National Center on Family Homelessness indicates that children experiencing
homelessness are twice as likely to be food insecure, and, according to the U.S. Department of Education, have three times the rate of emotional and behavioral problems. The National Alliance to End
Homelessness states that they experience more school mobility, have lower academic performance, and are more likely to experience separations from their families. Sixth Street Shelter works to keep
families housed together and to offer greater stability and a supportive environment to children and their families while they navigate the challenges they face. Homelessness is often traumatic for children,
and the Sixth Street Shelter provides resources and services to help mitigate these impacts and set families on a path out of homelessness.
Timeline
This project would be implemented upon receipt of funding and would be completed within 6 months, as long as there are no supply chain delays. As soon as funding is received, we would place an order for
furniture and appliances. The apartments receiving these items would be cleaned and painted while we awaited delivery. Once the furniture and appliances arrived, they would be installed in the
apartments. Finally, we would restock the apartment with other donated items, including food, kitchenware, bedding, and towels. Three fresh, bright, and fully stocked apartments would be ready for new
families to arrive and make their (temporary) home at the Sixth Street Shelter.
KFC Participation
KFC restaurant employees could participate in this project by volunteering to help with the apartment refresh process. This may include cleaning and painting apartments, as well as stocking each
apartment with items a family will need, such as food, kitchenware, bedding, and towels. They may also assist when the new furniture and appliances arrive, helping to arrange the items in the apartments to
create a warm, welcoming space for a family.
