Community Action of the Lehigh Valley
(CALV) was established in 1965 as a part of
Lyndon B. Johnson’s War on Poverty.  The
mission of CALV is to improve the quality of
life in the Lehigh Valley by building a
community in which all people have access
to economic opportunity, the ability to
pursue that opportunity, and a voice in the
decisions that affect their lives. Today, CALV
operates 13 programs and 3 subsidiary
corporations. As a Community Action
Agency (CAA), CALV is committed to
ensuring that the community plays an active
role in identifying needs, developing, and
implementing solutions to improve the
 

quality of life in the region. Toward that end,
CAAs have a tripartite Board of Directors
comprised of at least one-third of its
members from the low-income community,
one-third from the public sector, and one-
third from the private sector.
The mission of Second Harvest Food Bank of
the Lehigh Valley and Northeast
Pennsylvania, a program of Community
Action Lehigh Valley, is to obtain food and
distribute it to people in need through area
non-profits and to provide resources for
education and advocacy to end hunger. We
provide nutritious food, offer nutrition
education, advocate to end food insecurity,
dismantle barriers to create access, and work
to attain food justice. We respect and
preserve the dignity of each of our
neighbors.
Second Harvest began its mission to end
food insecurity in 1982, as the Lehigh Valley
Food Bank. Initially, distributions were made
from the back of a rented truck to a network
of about 15 nonprofit agencies. Since
September 2015, Second Harvest Food Bank
has been operating from a 65,000 square-
foot warehouse in East Allen Township.
Today, Second Harvest distributes 14+
million pounds of food a year to 200+
nonprofit agencies across Carbon, Lehigh,
Monroe, Northampton, Pike, and Wayne
Counties.  Last year, 41% of this food was
fresh produce, perishable protein, and fresh
dairy, and 1.9 million pounds of food was
rescued from ending up in a landfill. Second
Harvest also assists people with SNAP
(Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program)
applications; offers nutrition education to
promote self-sufficiency and positive health
outcomes; provides supplemental food to
nearly 2,500 low-income seniors each month;
distributes supplemental food to
approximately 560 military households a
month; and operates an incubator farm to
help train a new generation of farmers while
also increasing the amount of fresh food in
the local economy.
Collectively, the Second Harvest network

serves more than 100,000 people each
month. The pantries within the Second
Harvest network are operated almost solely
through the work of community volunteers.
Our food distribution is a model of
neighbors helping neighbors.
Provide a brief
description of the
project/activity.
Second Harvest Food Bank is committed to
providing the resource of food to people
who need it, with an emphasis on more
nutritious, fresh options. We know that the
first thing cut from a household budget
when funds are tight are healthy foods
(including fresh fruit and vegetables, eggs,
milk and other dairy, and meat), which are
more expensive than their highly processed
counterparts. Food insecurity and poverty
are social determinants of health and poor
health outcomes make it nearly impossible
for people to thrive. According to the Food
Research and Action Center (FRAC), “people
living in or near poverty have
disproportionately worse health outcomes
and less access to health care than those
who do not.”
Feeding America estimates that there are
over 5,140 food insecure people in Wayne
County, which is 10% of the population. Data
from Second Harvest last fiscal year indicates
that we served 2,768 Wayne County
residents. That means we are potentially only
reaching roughly 50% of people who need
food assistance. We strive to expand our
reach and provide more fresh, nutritious
foods to the people who need it most.  
As part of our larger nutritional strategy,
Second Harvest recently developed and
implemented a Food and Nutrition policy
based on Healthy Eating Research (HER)
guidelines (which were created in
partnership between the Robert Wood
Johnson Foundation, Duke University, and
the charitable food network). These
guidelines prioritize and deprioritize food by
categorizing them as “choose often”,
“choose sometimes”, and “choose rarely”.
Second Harvest will place an emphasis on
sourcing from local Wayne County growers
and producers when possible. We currently
partner with the following Wayne County

growers and producers to bring fresh, local
foods to our food pantries: Valley View Farm,
Willow Wisp Organic Farm, Lato Sud Farms,
and Creamworks Creamery, Inc. Funds from
the Wayne County CDBG program will allow
us to strengthen these partnerships to bring
more fresh foods to people in need.
Wayne County CDBG funds will be used to
purchase and distribute fresh, nutritious food
to our pantry network in Wayne County.

What specific community
needs does this
project/activity address?
This project addresses food insecurity of low-
income members of the community. The
need in Wayne County is great. In 2021, data
from Feeding America’s Map the Meal Gap
indicated that 5,140 people, or 10% of the
population in Wayne County was facing food
insecurity; that number increased to 12.7%
for children.
Populations facing food insecurity are
strongly correlated with those that have
been impacted by systemic racism and
economic exploitation. The food insecurity
rate across Wayne County is higher for Black
(14%) and Hispanic (19%) individuals, as
compared to their white counterparts (8%).
A pre-pandemic study found that following
the USDA’s MyPlate Dietary Guidelines
would cost a family of four between
$1,000-$1,200 a month ($12,000-$14,400
annually) depending on the age of the family
members. For a comparison, the average
middle-income family in the United States
spends roughly $6,224 on food each year
with the average lower-income family
spending even less at roughly $3,862 per
year. Eating healthy food consistently is
simply out of reach for many families.
Inflation continues to have a devastating
impact. According to the USDA, the cost of
groceries rose 11.8% from December 2021 to

December 2022. The cost of specific items
rose even higher – the price of eggs
increased by 60%.
The Second Harvest network of member
agencies continues to serve historic volumes
of people – currently 100,000+ each month.
Elevated food prices have caused many
families to turn to the charitable food
network for the first time. And often, the only
fresh, healthy foods that a family consumes
come from our food pantries - they simply
do not have access otherwise. Second
Harvest plays an integral role in ensuring all
people have access to the healthy foods
needed to thrive.
What specific geographic
area does this
project/activity serve?
This project will serve Wayne County.
According to the U.S. Census’ 2021 American
Community Survey 5-Year Estimates, the
population of Wayne County is 51,173 and
11% of people (and 15% of children) live in
poverty and 12% have relied on SNAP
benefits in the previous twelve months.
ALICE (Asset Limited, Income Constrained,
Employed) data paints a stark picture,
indicating that in 2021, 37% of families in
Wayne County were struggling to survive
(ALICE focuses on working families and the
perilous gap between thriving and surviving).
The Massachusetts Institute of Technology
Living Wage Calculator indicates that a living
wage in 2021 for a Wayne County household
consisting of one adult and two children is
$33.78 an hour ($70,262 annually). Median
household income in Wayne County is
$56,744, which illustrates the gap between a
living wage and not being able to afford
nutritious food and other necessities,
including appropriate housing and proper
clothing.
