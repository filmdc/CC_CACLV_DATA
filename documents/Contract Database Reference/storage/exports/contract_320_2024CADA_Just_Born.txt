Organization Background
 Primary Goals and Objectives 
 
Community Action Lehigh Valley was created in 1965 to work with and for low-income residents of
the Lehigh Valley to combat poverty. Community Action Development Allentown was incorporated on
September 7, 1993, as a subsidiary community and economic development corporation of
Community Action Lehigh Valley.
Community Action Development Allentown’s governing body is a Board of Directors made up of
neighborhood residents, representatives from local government, and professionals from the banking
community and local funding sources. By design, the Board draws half its membership from the
neighborhood and includes representatives from traditionally underrepresented groups. Relying
particularly on the life experiences and practical knowledge of neighborhood residents, the Board
sets policy, investigates and approves projects, and provides guidance for planning and
development. Strong community involvement on the Board of Directors leads to programming that
represents the best interests of the neighborhood.
Community Action Development Allentown has historically worked in the City of Allentown within a
focus area known as the downtown. Currently, there are 41,012 residents who live in the focus area
served by Community Action Development Allentown. The focus area is densely populated and home
to 32.7% of residents in the City of Allentown. The community is more racially and ethnically diverse
than the city overall; 68.3% of residents in the focus area are Hispanic/Latino and 15.2% are Black
or African American.
Community Action Development Allentown was recently approved for the fourth year of a six-year
Neighborhood Partnership Program (NPP) through the Pennsylvania Department of Community and
Economic Development. This NPP provides tax credits to 10 local companies that have committed to
contribute a collective $650,000 for six successive years. Community Action Development
Allentown’s most recent six-year Master Plan, Allentown’s Future, puts forth a comprehensive youth-
based strategy to provide opportunities for education, vocation, health, recreation, and citizenship to
the youth of Center City Allentown.

 Program/Project Title 
 
Youth Entrepreneurship Program
 
 What need in the community are you trying to address with this specific program? 
 
According to the American Community Survey Five-Year Estimates (2017-2021), the median
household income of $47,703 for the City of Allentown is significantly lower than that of the state at
$67,587 and Lehigh County at $69,440. Furthermore, household median income for the target
neighborhood was only $36,393. A total of 23.3% of the population in the City of Allentown lives
below the poverty level. In Pennsylvania, that figure was 11.8% and in Lehigh County, 12.1%. The
target neighborhood has the highest percentage living below the poverty level at 33.6% of the
population. All census tracts in the target neighborhood are considered by the U.S. Census Bureau
as “poverty areas,” which are defined as “census tracts or block numbering areas (BNA's) where at
least 20 percent of residents were below the poverty level.” According to the American Community
Survey Five-Year Estimates (2017-2021), the median unemployment rate for the target
neighborhood was 9.8%, again significantly higher than surrounding areas. In Lehigh County, the
unemployment rate was 6.1%, and in the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania it was 5.5%.
There are many hurdles to financial autonomy for youth in Allentown. The City of Allentown’s
“Allentown Vision 2030: Comprehensive & Economic Development Plan” includes principles of
increasing access to training and skill building as well as to foster small business and
entrepreneurship. According to the Allentown Vision 2030 plan, creating a robust entrepreneurial
ecosystem means ensuring that anyone can start and grow a business, regardless of income level.
Entrepreneurs and small businesses need resources and support to start their business, grow and
maintain that business, and obtain financial success. By providing comprehensive assistance to the
most economically vulnerable, especially those youth who do not wish to pursue a traditional college
degree but instead enter the workforce after graduating high school, Allentown’s youth will have
greater opportunity to achieve financial mobility and stability.

This program will provide business trainings and entrepreneurial mentorship opportunities for low-
income youth aged 15-21 residing in the City of Allentown, specifically the downtown/Center City
area. The entrepreneurship classes are held twice a week. One session per week features a small
business owner who presents their story about how they got started. The second weekly session
focuses on technical aspects of launching a business, including topics like setting up an LLC, filing for
a fictitious name, acquiring a business license, and opening a business account. The Youth
Entrepreneurship Program will offer multiple 7-week sessions both virtually and in-person, based
around the Allentown School District’s calendar to align with holidays and summer break.
 
 Please describe the population that will benefit from this program. 
 
In the Allentown School District, 56% of the student body identifies as Hispanic or Latino, and 11%
identifies as Black or African American. Nearly 80% of Allentown School District students come from
low-income families, and nearly 30% of families have incomed below the federal poverty level.
These students face challenges and trauma including intergenerational poverty, racism and
discrimination, and a lack of economic opportunity not seen in more affluent areas of the Lehigh
Valley.
Educational disparities are glaringly evident when comparing City of Allentown residents to other
residents of Lehigh County and the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania. Residents of the City of
Allentown have educational attainment levels that are significantly lower than Pennsylvania averages
in every category of degree completion: high school graduation, some college or associate degree,
bachelor’s degree, and graduate degree. These attainment levels are even more significant when
comparing the target neighborhood to the city. A much larger proportion of target neighborhood
residents (27.5%) did not complete high school in comparison to city residents (19.4%).
Dropout rates and graduation rates in Allentown are also cause for alarm. According to recent data
made available by the Pennsylvania Department of Education, Allentown School District’s dropout
rate of 4.1% is the highest in the Lehigh Valley—a rate more than double that of the second-highest
district.
A significant portion of the city’s teenagers and young adults are not enrolled in school or working,
leaving them with idle time and a need to find ways to earn money. In Allentown, 16.8% of those
aged 15 to 19 are not enrolled in school and 6.7% are completely “idle” meaning they are neither
enrolled in school nor employed. Further, there are clear racial disparities with regards to this
population. Only 1% of teens who identify as white alone are classified as idle, while that figure rises
to 7.2% and 8.2% for those who identify as Black or Hispanic/Latino, respectively. Areas with
historically high poverty and unemployment rates, coupled with low educational attainment and
access to opportunity, feed petty crime, violent crime, and gang recruitment.
Children in the community are affected by local violence, both directly and indirectly. The 2019
Community Health Needs Assessment (CHNA) conducted by Lehigh Valley Health Network (LVHN)
shows that of residents surveyed, 55.1% identified violence as an issue present in the community.
The CHNA indicates an even higher percentage response related to gang activity being present
(57.7%).
Based on data from the Lehigh County’s Regional Intelligence and Investigation Center (RIIC), as of
2015, there were 30 gangs operating in the City of Allentown and surrounding municipalities, with
70 subsets of those gangs. Unofficially, Allentown School District (ASD) staff are aware of heavy
recruitment in Allentown’s schools, beginning as early as second grade, and with at least five active
gangs at Francis D. Raub Middle School. School data, while technically anecdotal, has been
substantiated by staff at Promise Neighborhoods of the Lehigh Valley, a partner nonprofit who has
worked closely with the school administration for several years.
