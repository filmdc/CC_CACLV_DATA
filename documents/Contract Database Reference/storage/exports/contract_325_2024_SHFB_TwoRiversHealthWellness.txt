REPORT ON OUTCOMES AND RESULTS FOR 2022 - 2023 GRANT CYCLE 
Expanding Nutrition Education in Northampton County 
 
 
PROJECT SUMMARY 
 
Second Harvest Food Bank of the Lehigh Valley and Northeast Pennsylvania (SHFB) is focused 
on distributing fresh, nutritious food across our six-	county service area, including Northampton 
County, to people facing food insecurity. Providing nutrition information to our neighbors is a 
critical component of developing healthy communities. To guide our efforts, SHFB worked to 
develop a more comprehensive Food and Nutrition Policy, guided by nutrition educators.  
Additionally, SHFB was invested in providing additional educational resources to our community 
via our Share Our Strength’s Cooking Matters
® 
program, Military Share distributions and Senior 
Food Box programs. 
 
 
OUTCOMES AND RESULTS 
 
GOAL: Second Harvest will improve nutrition education for people with low-incomes.   
Performance Targets:   
A. Provide 36 nutrition education classes via Cooking Matters

.  (At least 20 classes will take

Between July 2022 – 	June 2023, SHFB nutrition educator, Tamyah Brice, held 50 Cooking 
Matters
®
 classes with a total of 387 participants; 21 classes were held in Northampton County, 
with 321 participants. Classes were held at senior centers, schools, community centers, shelters, 
education centers, and hospitals. The classes provided participants with food budgeting, easy-	to-
make recipes, and food safety education, with the goal of helping them develop long-	term, healthy 
eating habits. As SHFB looks to expand 	the program, we anticipate being able to provide 
participants with the ingredients used in class, so that they can truly bring the nutrition lesson home 
with them. 
 
In addition to our Cooking Matters

expanding their use of the healthy choices available to them. This program, part of the Healthy 
Pantry Initiative (HPI), provided samples of foods prepared with fresh foods, including apple 
cinnamon bread, vegetable chili, peanut butter and date energy bites, baked oatmeal, couscous 
with walnuts and dates and various fruit/vegetable smoothies. Nine HPI tastings were held in our 
service area, three of which were held in Northampton County; more than 430 neighbors engaged 
in a tasting, with 115 people visiting a Northampton location. 
 
Additionally, SHFB is including 	recipe cards (see example) and other helpful nutrition information 
with our Senior Box distributions, as well as our Military Share programs.

Performance Targets: 
A. Increase distribution through the Fresh Forward program of fresh and perishable product

We far exceeded our goal of increasing fresh, healthy food distribution in the county. From July 
1, 2022 through June 30, 2023 (FY 2023), SHFB distributed 1.62 million pounds of fresh 
produce, perishable protein, and fresh dairy to partner agencies in Northampton County – 
a 55% increase from FY2022 (1.03 million pounds). Since July 2023, Northampton County 
food pantries have also sourced 41,900 lbs. of fresh products directly from Lehigh Valley 
farms through our involvement with the Local Food Purchasing Agreement. Local products 
sourced for pantries included items like potatoes, cherry tomatoes, watermelons, sweet corn, 
plums, and many others. We look forward to continue growing our ability to provide these 
important, nutritious foods, coupled with effective community education and outreach.  
 
We not only distributed record amounts of food during the fiscal year, we served record numbers 
of people. On average, SHFB served 21,481 individuals per month in Northampton County 
during the fiscal year – 	a 62% increase over FY22 (average of 13,295 individuals per month). 
Over the last fiscal year, we saw the number of children being served by a Northampton 
County partner agency increase by 55%, and seniors by 58%. We saw 65% more families 
newly entering the charitable food system, measured by TEFAP self-declaration of need 
forms collected at partner agencies (7,561 households in FY22 vs. 12,513 households in 
FY23). Numbers continue to grow into FY24.

In June 2023, SHFB’s updated Food and Nutrition Policy was approved by the Board of Directors 
of Community Action Lehigh Valley, the parent organization of SHFB. This new policy will guide 
SHFB programs and staff in making consistent and informed decisions about food purchasing, 
procurement, and distribution in ways that protect and support individual and community health.  
 
The initiative, led by SHFB’s Nutrition Educators, ranked foods according to a three-tiered system: 
• Red – Choose Rarely 
• Yellow – Choose Sometimes 
• Green – Choose Often 
 
The rankings were developed using the Healthy Eating Research (HER) Nutrition Guidelines for 
Charitable Food Systems, the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, and research about neighbor food 
preferences.  These guidelines and research provide an evidence-	based approach to promoting 
healthy food access that is tailored to the unique needs and capacity of the charitable food system. 
 
Utilizing these rankings, SHFB began a phased approach to improving the nutritional quality of 
the foods we obtain and distribute. The first phase, completed in June 2023, limited purchases 
of food product by SHFB to those foods in only green or yellow categories. 	Foods in the red 
category or labeled as “unranked” are no longer purchased by SHFB. The second phase, currently 
in process and scheduled to be completed by December 2023, is to identify and rank the 
government commodities available to SHFB. The final phase will be to rank all donated product, 
with the end goal of maintaining an inventory of 15% or less designated as “Choose Rarely” or 
“red” foods. 
 
 
CHALLENGES 
 
The current economic climate continues to impact the neighbors in SHFB’s service area. Inflation 
continues to be a factor and is now exacerbated by the affordable housing crisis affecting the 
Greater Lehigh Valley. As the demand on the charitable food system increases, food banks, partner 
agencies, and other resources are themselves facing increased financial burdens. 
 
SHFB faced a significant challenge in reinvigorating our Health and Nutrition Plan initiative, due 
to a staffing shortage. During this grant period, SHFB was able to hire a new Nutrition Educator 
who has been successful in getting the plan approved and implemented.  
 
As neighbors look to stretch every dollar of their food budget, SHFB is aware that the nutritional 
quality of chosen foods may be impacted. It is our continued mission, through partnerships with 
organizations like Two Rivers, to not only increase the amount of food available to our 
communities but to improve the quality of that food.

The mission of Second Harvest Food Bank of the Lehigh Valley and Northeast Pennsylvania, a 
program of Community Action Lehigh Valley, is to obtain food and distribute it to people in need 
through area non- profits and to provide resources for education and advocacy to end hunger. We 
provide nutritious food, offer nutrition education, advocate to end food insecurity, dismantle 
barriers to create access, and work to attain food justice. We respect and preserve the dignity of 
each of our neighbors.  
 
Second Harvest Food Bank, a member of Feeding America – 	the nation’s largest charitable food 
network, serves Lehigh, Northampton, Carbon, Monroe, Pike, and Wayne counties. Last year, 
Second Harvest distributed 14.7 million pounds of food to our network of 200+ nonprofit agencies

a 44% increase from the previous year. Included in this total is 6.7 million pounds of fresh produce, 
perishable protein, and fresh dairy products (46% of total distribution). Annually, we rescue more 
than 3 million pounds of food from ending up in a landfill.  
 
On average, the Second Harvest network serves more than 87,000 people each month - 	a 52% 
increase from last year. Inflation continues to have a devasting impact and the need for nutrition 
education is greater than ever as more families are relying on food pantries to feed themselves.

 	Data from Feeding America’s Map the Meal Gap indicates that 7.8	% of the population in 
Northampton County was facing food insecurity; that number amplified to 9.2% for children. That 	means that 24,170 people, including 5,720 children, in Northampton County didn’t have adequate 
access to food that is safe, nutritious, and consistent with personal (including cultural) preferences.

7 
 
Populations facing food insecurity are strongly correlated with those that have been impacted by 
systemic racism and economic exploitation. The food insecurity rate across Northampton County 
is higher for Black (16%) and Hispanic (16%) individuals, as compared to white individuals (6%). 
 
The current economic climate continues to impact the neighbors in Second Harvest’s service area. 
Inflation continues to be a factor and is now exacerbated by the affordable housing crisis affecting 
the Greater Lehigh Valley. Monthly reports submitted by our partner agencies show that the 
number of people receiving food in Northampton County increased by 8% in the period of July 
2022 through June 2023, compared to the same period in the previous year	.  
 
According to the U.S. Census Bureau, 	the poverty rate in Northampton County is 11% (17% for 
children), meaning that 33,543 	people are living at or below the poverty level. This includes 10,277 
children. Additionally, the poverty rate for Black individuals is 16% and Hispanic or Latino 
individuals is 24%, as compared to the poverty rate of 8% for White individuals. Poverty, and 
therefore food insecurity, are social determinants of health. People living in low-income 
households are disproportionately diagnosed with diet-related illnesses.  
 
Lehigh Valley Health Network’s 2022 Community Health Needs Assessment Health Profile in 
Northampton County reported that 9% of Northampton County adults are diabetic and 31% are 
considered obese (which is higher than the national average of 29.5%). Additionally, according to 
The State of Childhood Obesity, an initiative of the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation, in 
Pennsylvania, 15.1% of youth ages 10 to 17 are obese. 
 
FRAC’s report titled, Hunger & Health: Impact of Poverty, Food Insecurity, and Poor Nutrition, 
cites that childhood poverty and socioeconomic inequalities have health implications that carry 
through into adulthood. For example, lower childhood socioeconomic status is associated with 
chronic disease, poor mental health, and unfavorable health behaviors in adulthood. Poverty in 
childhood has been linked to serious, long-	term economic consequences, including higher health 
care expenditures, lower educational achievement, and increased risk of poverty later in life. 
 
Poverty, food insecurity, and poor nutrition have serious consequences for the health and well-
being of children and adults, including a greater risk for chronic disease and poor mental health. 
Beyond the consequences for individuals and families, these consequences also have costly 
implications for the economy and healthcare system.  
 
A USDA Food and Nutrition Service study, SNAP Education and Evaluation Study (Wave II), 
showed that strong nutrition education can lead to healthier food choices by SNAP recipients, 
specifically elementary school children and seniors.  	The study found that children participating 
in nutrition education programs increased their daily fruit and vegetable intake at home and 	were 
more likely to choose low-fat or fat-free milk. Seniors also consumed more fruits and vegetables 
daily. The study demonstrates that effective nutrition education programs can impact SNAP 
participants’ willingness to try, buy, and eat more healthy foods. Nutrition education is a critical 
tool in increasing access to and consumption of nutritious foods.  
 
To broaden access and opportunity, it is essential that Second Harvest continues to provide 
nutritious foods and nutrition education to the residents of Northampton County.

PURPOSE OF THE PROPOSAL AS IT RELATES TO THE NEED/PROBLEM 
 
Second Harvest is committed to providing the resource of food and nutrition education to people 
who need it, with an emphasis on more nutritious, fresh options. Proper nutrition is a critical 
component to personal well-being and good health outcomes. From birth, the intake of vital 
nutrients is essential to the growth and development of a healthy individual. Good nutrition is 
crucial to a person’s physical and mental health, academic achievement, and economic 
productivity. 
 
The need for nutrition education
 
is greater than ever as more individuals and families are relying 
on food pantries and low-cost food items to sustain a healthy life. Second Harvest’s nutrition 
education curriculum is designed to help families learn how to prepare nutritious food on a very 
limited budget. By integrating nutrition education and leveraging the expertise of our in-	house 
nutrition education staff, we can move forward with confidence that we are providing foods that 
will contribute to healthier lifestyles and provide people with the tools and knowledge necessary 
to incorporate lifestyle changes into their food consumption and preparation habits.
