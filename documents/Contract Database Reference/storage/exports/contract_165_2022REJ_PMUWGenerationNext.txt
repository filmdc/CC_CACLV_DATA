registration, it likely owns common law rights which are established simply by using the mark as 
part of its business. Common law rights provide the owner with the exclusive right to use a mark 
within a certain geographic area based on their priority use of the mark. When a trademark owner 
is the first to use a mark, they are referred to as the “senior user.” Anyone who starts using a 
confusingly similar mark thereafter is called the “junior user.” A senior user of a common law 
trademark can sue a junior user if the junior user is operating in the same geographic area and 
using the mark in connection with the same or related goods and/or services. 
 
Thus, since Community Action Lehigh Valley (“CALV”) is limited to providing its 
services under the mark GENERATION NEXT within the Lehigh Valley and has no 
intention to expand or move to the geographic territory of Generation Next (Minneapolis), 
there is no problem from an infringement standpoint.  
 
Likewise, if Generation Next (Minnesota) decided to move to or expand into the Lehigh 
Valley, CALV would be the senior user of the mark GENERATION NEXT and could prevent 
Generation Next (Minneapolis) from providing the same or similar services under the same or a 
similar mark in the Lehigh Valley.  
 
As mentioned above, Generation Next (Minneapolis) has not filed an application to 
federally register its mark GENERATION NEXT. Therefore, its rights are geographically limited 
to the area in which it uses the mark.  
 
On the other hand, if CALV were to file an application and obtain a federal registration for 
the mark GENERATION NEXT, it would enjoy the following benefits:   
 
1) Discourages others from using confusingly similar marks in the first place by making 
the mark easy to find in a trademark availability search, thereby preventing problems before they 
even begin.  
 
2) Protects against registration of confusingly similar marks, as the Trademark Office has 
a duty to cite prior registrations against applications for confusingly similar marks and to refuse to 
register such marks, thereby enlisting the power of the US Government in helping to prevent 
infringement at no additional cost to you.  
 
3) Treats the mark as if used nationwide as of the application date, which is vital in a system 
in which first use wins. Otherwise, your rights are limited to the geographic area of use or 
reputation of the mark, potentially allowing others to use the same mark in another geographic 
area and leading to inevitable confusion when one or both parties expands.  
 
4) Provides nationwide notice of ownership of the mark as of the registration date, 
preventing others from claiming their subsequent adoption of the mark was in "good faith."  
 
5) Serves as evidence of the validity and exclusive ownership of the mark for the goods 
and services listed in the registration, with heightened protection after five years and the possibility
